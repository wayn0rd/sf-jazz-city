# The Rhythms of the City: A History of San Francisco Jazz

San Francisco's relationship with jazz goes back nearly to the music's beginning. From the dance halls of the Barbary Coast to the after-hours rooms of the Fillmore, from Beat poetry readings in North Beach to the glass-walled stage of the SFJAZZ Center, the city has treated jazz less as entertainment than as a language — one for talking about freedom, experiment, and survival.

Unlike New York or Los Angeles, San Francisco never really ran the jazz business. Its scene lived at the edges instead: waterfront saloons, neighborhood clubs, basement jam sessions, union halls, churches. The history isn't a straight line. It's a series of revivals, migrations, reinventions, and losses — the story of a city that keeps remaking itself, sometimes at real cultural cost.

To tell it honestly is to tell a larger story about who gets to shape a city, who gets pushed out of it, and how music holds onto what redevelopment and fashion try to erase.

## Terrific Street

The story begins in the Barbary Coast, the red-light district near today's Jackson Square, whose main drag earned the nickname "Terrific Street" for its saloons and round-the-clock nightlife. By the 1910s its cafés had become meeting grounds for ragtime, blues, marching band music, and the syncopated dance rhythms that people were just starting to call jazz — or "jass," depending on who was spelling it.

The dean of that scene was Sid LeProtti, a Black pianist and bandleader whose So Different Jazz Band, the house act at Purcell's So Different Café, grew from piano-and-drums into a full ensemble with clarinet, saxophone, trombone, and banjo. LeProtti's men played short, hot sets for dancers who paid in brass tokens, and the dancing mattered as much as the playing — San Francisco helped launch the Texas Tommy, a wild, athletic dance that looked forward to swing.

The city drew New Orleans royalty early, too. Jelly Roll Morton lived here from roughly 1917 to 1922, running the Jupiter bar and bragging that he'd poached LeProtti's best musicians. King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band played Oakland in 1921; Kid Ory worked the area as well. Already the city was showing its defining habit: absorb influences from everywhere, mix them, and hand back something restless and new.

It didn't last. Vice crackdowns starting around 1913, then Prohibition, then the Depression tamed the Barbary Coast. Jazz drifted upward into the respectable hotel orchestras of Nob Hill — the Fairmont, the Palace — and into vaudeville houses. The music survived, but as swing and then bebop remade jazz elsewhere, San Francisco's early sound slipped out of the national conversation.

## The Revival at the Dawn Club

By the early 1940s most of the country had embraced the polished sound of big band swing. San Francisco, contrarian as ever, went the other way.

At the Dawn Club, in the Monadnock Building south of Market, trumpeter Lu Watters and his Yerba Buena Jazz Band — with the great trombonist Turk Murphy beside him — rejected slick stock arrangements in favor of the raw, collective sound of early New Orleans jazz, learned off rare 78s by Armstrong, Morton, and Oliver.

Their music was loud, polyphonic, and communal. Instead of one soloist gliding over a sleek chart, you got cornet, clarinet, trombone, piano, banjo, tuba, and drums all speaking at once — a joyful argument. The revival made San Francisco a national magnet for musicians and listeners convinced that jazz's future ran through its past. It was rebellion staged as preservation, which may be the most San Francisco gesture there is.

## The Fillmore

The postwar Fillmore District became "the Harlem of the West," and it holds the emotional center of this whole history. But the story can't be told honestly without what came before.

Before World War II, the neighborhood was Japantown — Nihonmachi — home to a thriving Japanese American community whose shops, churches, and homes lined the same blocks that would soon ring with jazz. In 1942, under Executive Order 9066, the federal government forced Japanese Americans off the West Coast and into internment camps. Within weeks the Fillmore stood emptied of its residents.

At that same moment, Black workers were pouring into the Bay Area for wartime shipyard jobs at Hunters Point, Marin City, and Richmond. The housing that interned families had been forced to abandon became one of the few places in segregated San Francisco where Black families could rent. One community's catastrophe opened the door to another community's foothold — and when Japanese Americans returned after the war, many found their homes and businesses gone for good. Everything that flourished afterward grew out of that violently disturbed soil.

And what flourished was extraordinary. The Fillmore wasn't just a place where jazz got performed; it was an ecosystem. Clubs, hotels, restaurants, churches, barbershops, record stores, and after-hours rooms all fed the music. Jack's Tavern, the Blue Mirror, the Champagne Supper Club, the Texas Playhouse — they thrived along Fillmore, Sutter, Post, and Buchanan. Touring musicians played their formal downtown engagements, then headed into the neighborhood late at night, where the sets stretched past closing and the line between performer and audience got wonderfully thin.

The most legendary room was Jimbo's Bop City, which Jimbo Edwards ran from around 1950 to 1965 in a space that had briefly been Slim Gaillard's "Vout City." An after-hours joint that famously sold pie and coffee instead of liquor, Bop City hosted Miles Davis, Charlie Parker, Billie Holiday, John Coltrane, and Dexter Gordon, who mingled and sparred with local players like John Handy and Pony Poindexter until dawn. There, bebop could breathe. Musicians could stretch out, compete, commune.

Then the city tore it apart. Starting in the 1950s and accelerating through the '60s, urban renewal projects run by the San Francisco Redevelopment Agency under Justin Herman leveled whole blocks of the Fillmore. Families were displaced; Black-owned businesses were destroyed. The city didn't just lose buildings — it fractured the cultural network that had sustained one of the most important jazz communities on the West Coast.

Any honest history has to hold three truths at once: the brilliance of what was created there, the injustice that made the neighborhood available in the first place, and the civic failure that later helped destroy it.

## The Blackhawk and the Jazz Workshop

While the Fillmore burned bright after hours, two other rooms made the city indispensable to modern jazz.

The Blackhawk, at Turk and Hyde in the Tenderloin, ran from 1949 to 1963 — a scruffy dive with a famous chicken-wire partition that let underage listeners hear the music without legally being "in" the bar. Dizzy Gillespie, Art Blakey, Stan Getz, Lester Young, Gerry Mulligan, Cal Tjader, and the Modern Jazz Quartet all played there, and the room's smoke-thick intimacy was preserved forever on a run of important live records — most famously Miles Davis's *In Person: Friday and Saturday Nights at the Blackhawk* in 1961. Thelonious Monk was in town during those years too, recording *Thelonious Alone in San Francisco* at Fugazi Hall in 1959. That's how dense the city's modern jazz moment had become.

A few blocks north, the Jazz Workshop opened in North Beach in 1957 and became the West Coast home for extended residencies by Charles Mingus, Max Roach, Cannonball Adderley, Ornette Coleman, and Roland Kirk — Cannonball's *Live at the Jazz Workshop* catches the room at its peak. It also entered free-speech history when Lenny Bruce was arrested there on obscenity charges in 1961, a reminder that San Francisco's jazz rooms were never just musical spaces. They were places where the city argued with itself about what expression it would tolerate.

Together with Bop City, these rooms formed a loose nocturnal circuit: the Blackhawk gave touring giants a recording-grade stage, the Jazz Workshop hosted the avant-garde and the outspoken, and Bop City held the unofficial late-night summit after the curtains fell everywhere else.

## The Beats and the Cool School

North Beach in the 1950s was the scene's intellectual laboratory. The Beat writers heard in jazz a model for everything they wanted from language — spontaneity, freedom, contempt for polite convention. Lawrence Ferlinghetti and Kenneth Rexroth staged poetry-and-jazz performances that treated the saxophone and the bass as extensions of the speaking voice. Jazz became a method of thinking: improvisational, searching, alive to the moment.

The Bay Area also produced its own cooler, more cerebral strain. Dave Brubeck, who studied under Darius Milhaud at Mills College, formed his famous quartet with alto saxophonist Paul Desmond, whose "Take Five" carried odd-meter jazz into the American mainstream. Vince Guaraldi later brought that Bay Area sound into millions of living rooms through *A Charlie Brown Christmas*. Cal Tjader's Latin jazz fed back into the Mission District's Afro-Cuban tradition, while bassist Vernon Alley and drummer Earl Watkins anchored the local rhythm sections for decades.

In 1965, Duke Ellington premiered his *Concert of Sacred Music* at Grace Cathedral on Nob Hill. Jazz had traveled from the Barbary Coast's brass-token dance floors to the vaults of the city's great church — foggy, thoughtful, urbane, and still quietly experimental.

## Survival and Fusion

The Blackhawk closed in 1963, Bop City in 1965, and the Fillmore's destruction ground on. But the music didn't end; it moved. Over the next decades it survived in smaller clubs, community spaces, churches, festivals, and neighborhood rooms.

The most important was Keystone Korner, which Todd Barkan ran from 1972 to 1983 in a former police bar in North Beach. For a decade it was among the most vital jazz rooms in the country — Stan Getz, McCoy Tyner, Bill Evans, and Art Blakey all recorded essential live albums there, and the club carried the Blackhawk's spirit into a new era.

Around it, the scene widened. Latin jazz rooted itself in the Mission. Fusion, free improvisation, funk-inflected grooves, straight-ahead bebop, and spiritual jazz all found audiences. Community organizations, cultural centers, educators, and radio — KCSM still broadcasts jazz around the clock from San Mateo — kept the music circulating and passed it to new hands. The Bay Area's openness to Latin American, Asian, African, and experimental influences gave the local sound a genuinely global character.

This stretch of the story matters precisely because it's less glamorous. It proves San Francisco jazz was never just a vanished golden age. Through displacement, changing tastes, and rising rents, the music kept adapting. It kept finding rooms.

## SFJAZZ and the Present

Today the history is honored in architecture as well as memory. The SFJAZZ Center, opened in 2013 at Franklin and Fell in Hayes Valley, is widely described as the first freestanding building in the country built specifically for jazz performance and education — a permanent civic home for the music. The organization traces back to Jazz in the City, founded in 1983, which grew into the San Francisco Jazz Festival before becoming SFJAZZ. Its stages now host the SFJAZZ Collective, global masters, students, and cross-genre experiments.

But the living scene is bigger than one building. It continues at the Black Cat in the Tenderloin, Keys Jazz Bistro in North Beach, Bird & Beckett Books and Records in Glen Park, the Boom Boom Room on Fillmore Street, and at the annual Fillmore Jazz Festival and the summer concerts at Stern Grove.

## Coda

San Francisco jazz has always lived between memory and reinvention: the rough pulse of the Barbary Coast, the collective fire of the revival, the brilliance and heartbreak of the Fillmore, the recorded intimacy of the Blackhawk, the searching edge of the Jazz Workshop, the Beats' restless talk, the institutional confidence of SFJAZZ.

The city has not always protected the people who made the music possible. The Japanese families removed from Nihonmachi, the Black families displaced from the Fillmore, the musicians who watched their rooms get bulldozed — they belong to this history as much as any record date does.

From waterfront dance halls to a chicken-wire dive bar to a concert hall built for the music, San Francisco has done more than play jazz. At its best, it has lived the music's central lesson: listen closely, respond honestly, and keep the song moving.
